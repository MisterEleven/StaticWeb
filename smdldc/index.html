    1Intro
    The brief

    Predict heat islands from aerial images

    • Train image-to-image models on data from map.bern.ch.
    • Compare the performance of different vision models.
    • Analyse how the models perform outside of Bern.

    Takeaways the supervisors are after: working with image2image models, and evaluating performance and generalisability. Supervisors: Rafael Bischof, Andreas Müller.

    Leakage trap

    A random split over 00All* puts a tile in training and its mirror image in the test set. The scores would measure memorisation. We work from the core set only and flip during training.

    How we analysed it

    Eight passes over the data

    Each pass answered a question the previous one raised.

    1. Colour census

      Counted every RGB value across all 2,275 heatmaps (149 M pixels, 2,311 distinct colours). Twelve colours cover 90% of pixels and the rest are anti-aliasing and drawn-on street lines.

    2. Snap to palette

      Assigned each pixel to its nearest main colour and flagged anything more than 12 RGB units away as overlay. About 95% of pixels per tile snap cleanly.

    3. Order the classes

      First estimate: the mean greenness of the photo under each colour. Then the map.bern.ch legend arrived, and we fitted it directly as a semi-transparent blend.

    4. Reconstruct the layout

      Compared the edge pixels of neighbouring ids. id+1 continues to the right, and id+65 sits directly below in 200 of 200 samples. The tiles form one stitched map.

    5. Level distribution

      Mapped every pixel to one of the 14 temperature levels and compared the core and Large sets.

    6. Fit the rendering per tile

      Tiles were exported with different layer opacities (0.50 to 0.73) and, in three tiles, a darker building grey. Each tile gets its own fitted palette before decoding.

    7. Find the heat raster

      Fitted one grid of cell boundaries over the whole stitched map. 98.55% of cells contain a single colour, so the cells are the true units of the data.

    8. Check buildings against the photo

      Compared the heatmap's building outlines with what the photo shows, in both directions: outlines with no building, and buildings with no outline.

    Finding 1

    The tiles are one continuous map of Bern

    Tile idN sits at row (N−1) // 65, column (N−1) % 65. Stitched together, the 2,275 tiles cover the city from the Wohlensee loop in the west to the Aare bend around the old town. Drag the slider to compare photo and heat map.

    Finding 2

    The pastel colours are the official legend at 60% opacity

    The tiles show softer colours than the legend. Blending each legend swatch with a near-white background (0.6 · legend + 0.4 · 250) and matching it to the observed colours gives a one-to-one assignment with a mean error of 12.5 RGB units. So the target is an ordered temperature scale, not a set of unrelated classes.

    LevelRangeLegendIn tilesFit errorWärmebelastung

    The worst fit is > 43 °C (error 39.8): dark purple loses the most in a blend. It is still the only plausible partner, and it covers just 0.7% of pixels.

    Finding 3

    The temperatures cluster in two groups

    Excluding buildings and overlay pixels, over half of the area sits at 23–29 °C (forest, parks, water edges). A second peak sits at 38–42 °C (sealed surfaces, rail, open fields). The middle levels are thin. This shape decides the loss: a plain mean-squared regression would hedge towards 32 °C where it is unsure, a value that is rare in the data.

    The core and Large sets have nearly the same distribution (at most 0.4 percentage points apart per level), so training on one scale and testing on the other is a fair experiment. Heat raster cells measure 9.45 px in core tiles; the Large tiles mix 7.55 and 11.35 px.

    Finding 4

    Where photo and heat map disagree

    Finding 5

    The heat map is a grid of 9.45 px cells, so lines and labels can be painted over

    The heat values were computed on a coarse raster and then drawn with street lines and names on top. Fitting one grid over the stitched map gives cells of 9.45 px in both directions. With that grid, 98.55% of cells hold a single colour.

    The cleaning rule follows from it. A pixel that already matches a legend colour keeps it. A pixel that does not (line, label, anti-aliasing) takes the most common level of its cell. That changes no correctly coloured pixel. Before cleaning, 4.29% of pixels were unreadable; after, 0.077% remain unknown, all in places where the source map has no value.

    Not every tile was rendered the same way

    A first version assumed one palette for all tiles and decoded half of id 382 as unknown, although its buildings are plainly visible. Fitting the layer opacity per tile shows several export batches: 1,411 tiles at 0.64, 360 at 0.55, 96 at 0.63, and a few others. Tiles at the common opacity are unaffected by the fix (0.01% of pixels change). Anyone downloading more tiles later should expect the same variation.

    One raster for the whole city

    The grid's offset moves by about 0.85 px from one tile to the next (256 mod 9.45). The city's heat map was rasterised once and then cut into tiles, so every cell boundary can be computed from a tile's row and column.

    Finding 6

    Some buildings exist in only one of the two images

    The building outlines in the heat map and the photo were made at different times. Some outlines sit on open fields (planned or newer buildings), and some buildings in the photo have no outline, so the heat map gives them a temperature.

    A first check looked at vegetation under each of the 14,241 outlines. 515 outlines (1.06% of building area) are more than 60% vegetated. That check also catches green roofs, which are real buildings. For a reliable flag in both directions, a small building detector (a U-Net) is trained on the photos in 5 spatial folds, so each tile is judged by a model that never saw its neighbourhood. It reaches a building IoU of 0.62 to 0.66 per fold. Where it confidently disagrees with the heat map, the area is flagged: an outline whose mean building probability is below 0.15, or a region above 0.85 with no outline.

    How flagged areas are used

    Flagged regions and a ring of 6 px around them are excluded from training and evaluation, like unknown pixels. The heat next to a mismatch was modelled with a different building layout than the photo shows.

    Finding 7

    Surface colour carries heat, and some of it disagrees with the photo

    Using simple colour rules on the photo (green vegetation, brown or bare, grey sealed), the heat level of each surface differs a lot. Mean level is 3.6 over green vegetation, 6.5 over grey sealed surfaces and 8.0 over brown or bare ground.

    Two kinds of disagreement stand out. Green and hot: the photo shows vegetation, but the heat is 40 to 41 °C or hotter. This is 7.1% of heat pixels, mostly crop fields. Brown and cool: the photo shows bare ground, but the heat is 26 °C or cooler. This is 0.13%.

    A disagreement is a suspicion, not an error. The heat may have been computed on another date, when the field was bare. The colour rule is also crude: brown catches roofs and soil alike.

    Using it in training

    Two masks are prepared, so each model can choose. The base mask ignores 0.80% of pixels. The land-cover mask also ignores the two conflict types, which brings it to 7.01%. Excluding them removes the farmland examples most needed for learning, so the choice should be tested rather than assumed.

    Manual review: a tile-by-tile viewer shows the flags next to the photo and the heat map. Images there are from map.bern.ch (City of Bern).

    How we could go forth

    Three output formulations, compared on equal terms

    All three use the same backbone family (U-Net first; DeepLabV3+ and SegFormer later) and the same split, photos and metrics. They differ only in the output head and loss. Which one is used in the final result depends on what the comparison shows.

    Track 3 · Chenjie

    Ordinal

    Output
    13 "hotter than level k" sigmoids (CORAL / CORN)
    Loss
    Sum of binary cross-entropies
    Metric
    MAE in °C, ±1-level accuracy

    Respects the order of the levels and does not average across the two peaks. A one-level miss costs little, a ten-level miss costs a lot.

    Side tasks, also open: an RGB regression (pix2pix-style) reference, and a search for an aerial image closer in date to the heat map. Both are compared against the same metrics.

    Shared evaluation

    Reported for every model: MAE in °C, accuracy within ±1 level, and accuracy on the five Wärmebelastung categories, which non-specialists can read. Metrics are also computed at the heat map's own grid resolution, since pixel-exact borders are not reachable.

    4Comparisons
    Task A · classification

    A pretrained encoder reaches about 2 °C mean error

    Each model predicts the heat level of every pixel, and we report the error in °C. The split is five spatial folds of 5 × 5 tile blocks, shared by all runs. Every number is the mean over the five folds, with the spread across folds after it. The colour-only baseline is kept in every comparison.

    What the seeds tell us

    The result is stable: four seeds give the same mean within 0.02 °C, so the 1.98 °C figure is not a lucky draw. The error is not spread evenly, though. Folds 0–2 average 2.05 °C and folds 3–4 average 1.86 °C; the spread between seeds is at most 0.05 °C on any single fold. The largest errors sit in the high-PET areas with green cover (see Finding 7), which is where the model is weakest.

    What the errors look like

    Errors come in clusters: whole neighbourhoods are off together. The model gets the broad pattern (forest and roads cool, sealed and built areas warm) but blurs fine detail. Its misses go both ways: too cool on the western fields and some built-up tiles, too hot on some sealed yards. On the busiest built-up tiles the error is about 1.5 °C higher than in the rest of the city, so the 1.5 °C goal is not met where the city is densest.

    The model never predicts the hottest level. Above 43 °C it outputs 0.00% of pixels, against 0.77% in the true map. At 42–43 °C it outputs 0.15%, against 2.22%. Below 23 °C it outputs 0.35%, against 2.14%. Its extremes are pulled into the middle, so the hottest areas are always underestimated. This is a limit of the model, not noise in the data.

    Evaluation design

    Splitting by spatial block

    The tiles are neighbours on one map, and neighbours look alike. A random split would put near-twins on both sides and flatter the scores somewhat. Our plan:

    • Cut the 65 × 35 grid into 5 × 5-tile blocks and assign whole blocks at random to train, validation and test (about 70 / 15 / 15).
    • Use the Large tiles as a measured transfer test: a different scale, with ground truth.
    • Use aerial imagery from outside Bern (for example swisstopo SWISSIMAGE) for the generalisation task. Without heat maps there, this check is qualitative, unless another city publishes a comparable map.

    The in-Bern test asks "how good on unseen tiles of this city". The outside imagery asks "does it work elsewhere at all". The report needs both, and only the first produces error numbers.

    5Fazit and next steps
    Limitations

    What the data cannot tell us

    HighPhoto and heat map from different times

    Fields that look green are labelled 40 °C. Seasonal and land-use mismatch sets a ceiling on achievable accuracy, especially in the agricultural west.

    HighNo ground truth outside Bern

    Generalisation to other cities can only be judged visually unless we find a comparable heat-stress map elsewhere.

    MediumOne city, one map

    2,275 tiles from one city cover few building styles and little topography. The effective number of independent samples is smaller than the tile count.

    MediumUnclear physical quantity

    "Wärmebelastung" in °C suggests a modelled thermal-comfort index such as PET, not measured surface temperature. The layer metadata should confirm the quantity, the time of day and the model used.

    MediumQuantised and overlaid target

    Values exist only as 14 bins in 9.45 px cells. Street lines and labels can be painted over, but a few large patches stay unknown. Bin edges are uneven (3 °C wide at the cool end, 1 °C at the hot end).

    MediumImbalanced levels

    23–26 °C covers 37% of the area and > 43 °C covers 0.7%. Extreme heat is the most relevant level and the rarest one.

    MediumBuildings are masked

    Roofs carry no temperature in this layer, so the model never learns anything about them. Predictions on buildings must be masked out at evaluation too.

    MediumBuildings from different dates

    Outlines and photo disagree in places. The flags come from a learned detector (IoU about 0.64), so a few mismatches will be missed and some small real buildings flagged.

    MediumReverse-engineered decoding

    The palette-to-level mapping comes from a colour fit, not from source data, and tiles were exported at different opacities. Each tile's palette is fitted separately. Raw values from the city would remove this step.
