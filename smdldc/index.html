    Research overview

    Result in brief

    How well can aerial images reconstruct Bern’s heat-stress map?

    We predict the 14-level heat-stress map of Bern from photos and compare three approaches, named by what the model outputs: Classification (one of 14 classes), Ordinal (13 ordered yes/no outputs) and Regression (one number in °C). With the 2021 photos the best Classification model reaches a mean error of 1.931 °C (median decoding, 20 runs), against 1.978 °C on the original photos. Argmax MAE improved in 20 of 20 paired runs; the median results improve in aggregate but are not established as paired seed-by-seed comparisons. Ordinal with the CORN head ties Classification on the original photos (1.976 against 1.975 °C). Regression with height, water and separate cast-shadow channels (sun at 10:00, 14:00 and 18:00) reaches 1.63 °C over five folds and two seeds, scored against exact degrees (a different, continuous scoring scale than the 14-level scoring used by Classification). The goal was 1.5 °C. Later test-guided tuning means the reported scores are development estimates, not an untouched final evaluation.

    Three weaknesses shape every number below. The models barely reach the hot end: The mapped median classification and independent-threshold models predict almost no pixels at 43 °C or more, Regression does for about a tenth of the pixels that are that hot (0.71% of the exact target; see the hot-end chapter). The 2021 photos were chosen by date and forest colour and rank second on structure in the displayed ten-tile comparison. And photo capture and target conditions can differ, so data uncertainty can contribute to the measured error, without establishing its share.

    Understanding and reconstructing the heat target

    From a rendered map to usable labels: palette, distribution, overlays, buildings, conflict flags and the exact source raster.

    What does the heat target measure?

    The target is physiological equivalent temperature (PET) from Bern’s modelled 2020 daytime heat-stress product, at 14:00. The unit is °C, but PET is not measured air temperature or remotely sensed surface temperature.

    The initial investigation used web-map screenshots with fourteen ordered colours, buildings and street overlays. Later, the city’s raw 5 m GeoTIFF made exact-degree regression possible. Both representations are part of the research; they have different validity masks and evaluation scales.

    Two target families

    Screenshot levels: palette reconstruction, coarse rendered cells and level-midpoint scoring.

    Source raster: exact PET degrees placed on the same tile footprints, excluding raster no-data and buildings.

    Do not rank models across these targets as if only the model changed.

    How we analysed it

    Eight passes over the data

    Each pass answered a question the previous one raised.

    1. Colour census

      Counted every RGB value across all 2,275 heatmaps (149 M pixels, 2,311 distinct colours). Twelve colours cover 90% of pixels and the rest are anti-aliasing and drawn-on street lines.

    2. Snap to palette

      Assigned each pixel to its nearest main colour and flagged anything more than 12 RGB units away as overlay. About 95% of pixels per tile snap cleanly.

    3. Order the classes

      First estimate: the mean greenness of the photo under each colour. Then the map.bern.ch legend arrived, and we fitted it directly as a semi-transparent blend.

    4. Reconstruct the layout

      Compared the edge pixels of neighbouring ids. id+1 continues to the right, and id+65 sits directly below in 200 of 200 samples. The tiles form one stitched map.

    5. Level distribution

      Mapped every pixel to one of the 14 temperature levels and compared the core and Large sets.

    6. Fit the rendering per tile

      Tiles were exported with different layer opacities (0.50 to 0.73) and, in three tiles, a darker building grey. Each tile gets its own fitted palette before decoding.

    7. Find the heat raster

      Fitted one grid of cell boundaries over the whole stitched map. 98.55% of cells contain a single colour, so the cells are the true units of the data.

    8. Check buildings against the photo

      Compared the heatmap's building outlines with what the photo shows, in both directions: outlines with no building, and buildings with no outline.

    Finding 2

    The pastel colours are the official legend at 60% opacity

    The tiles show softer colours than the legend. Blending each legend swatch with a near-white background (0.6 · legend + 0.4 · 250) and matching it to the observed colours gives a one-to-one assignment with a mean error of 12.5 RGB units. So the target is an ordered temperature scale, not a set of unrelated classes. The five categories in the last column are shown with English names; on map.bern.ch they read keine, schwach, mässig, stark and extrem Wärmebelastung.

    Finding 3

    The temperatures cluster in two groups

    Excluding buildings and overlay pixels, over half of the area sits at 23–29 °C (forest, parks, water edges). A second peak sits at 38–42 °C (sealed surfaces, rail, open fields). The middle levels are thin. This shape decides the loss: a plain mean-squared regression would hedge towards 32 °C where it is unsure, a value that is rare in the data.

    Finding 5

    The heat map is a grid of 9.45 px cells, so lines and labels can be painted over

    The heat values were computed on a coarse raster and then drawn with street lines and names on top. Fitting one grid over the stitched map gives cells of 9.45 px in both directions. With that grid, 98.55% of cells hold a single colour.

    The cleaning rule follows from it. A pixel that already matches a legend colour keeps it. A pixel that does not (line, label, anti-aliasing) takes the most common level of its cell. That changes no correctly coloured pixel. Before cleaning, 4.29% of pixels were unreadable; after, 0.077% remain unknown, all in places where the source map has no value.

    Not every tile was rendered the same way

    A first version assumed one palette for all tiles and decoded half of id 382 as unknown, although its buildings are plainly visible. Fitting the layer opacity per tile shows several export batches: 1,411 tiles at 0.64, 360 at 0.55, 96 at 0.63, and a few others. Tiles at the common opacity are unaffected by the fix (0.01% of pixels change). Anyone downloading more tiles later should expect the same variation.

    One raster for the whole city

    The grid's offset moves by about 0.85 px from one tile to the next (256 mod 9.45). The city's heat map was rasterised once and then cut into tiles, so every cell boundary can be computed from a tile's row and column.

    Finding 6

    Some buildings exist in only one of the two sources

    The building outlines in the heat map and the photo were made at different times. Some outlines sit on open fields (planned or newer buildings), and some buildings in the photo have no outline, so the heat map gives them a temperature.

    A first check looked at vegetation under each of the 14,241 outlines. 515 outlines (1.06% of building area) are more than 60% vegetated. That check also catches green roofs, which are real buildings. For a reliable flag in both directions, a small building detector (a U-Net) is trained on the photos in 5 spatial folds, so each tile is judged by a model that never saw its neighbourhood. It reaches a building IoU of 0.62 to 0.66 per fold. Where it confidently disagrees with the heat map, the area is flagged: an outline whose mean building probability is below 0.15, or a region above 0.85 with no outline.

    How flagged areas are used

    Flagged regions and a ring of 6 px around them are excluded from training and evaluation, like unknown pixels. The heat next to a mismatch was modelled with a different building layout than the photo shows.

    Finding 7

    Surface colour carries heat, and some of it disagrees with the photo

    Using simple colour rules on the photo (green vegetation, brown or bare, grey sealed), the heat level of each surface differs a lot. Mean level is 3.6 over green vegetation, 6.5 over grey sealed surfaces and 8.0 over brown or bare ground.

    Two kinds of disagreement stand out. Green and hot: the photo shows vegetation, but the heat is 40 to 41 °C or hotter. This is 7.1% of heat pixels, mostly crop fields. Brown and cool: the photo shows bare ground, but the heat is 26 °C or cooler. This is 0.13%.

    A disagreement is a suspicion, not an error. The heat may have been computed on another date, when the field was bare. The colour rule is also crude: brown catches roofs and soil alike.

    Two masks are prepared, so each model can choose. The base ignore flags cover 0.80% of raw pixels, in addition to the separate building exclusion. The land-cover mask adds the two conflict types, bringing the additional ignored share to 7.01% of raw pixels; buildings are still excluded separately.     Placing the label grid on the 5 m GeoTIFF

    The city's own raster has 5 m cells, finer than the 9.45 px heat cells in our tiles. To use it, each tile has to be put on the right spot of the GeoTIFF (LV95 coordinates).     Aerial imagery and temporal alignment

    Photo provenance is an experiment in its own right: acquisition, footprints, structural matching, colour, dates and the 2020 follow-up.

    Imagery · provenance and acquisition

    Recovering a photo source without recovering its date

    The supplied Bern photos came with enough information to identify a source family, but not enough to reconstruct their acquisition. SWISSIMAGE 10 cm from swisstopo is the recorded source. The capture year of the supplied copies was not recorded. Their PNG files carried no coordinates, and the October check found no world files or tile index in the supplied image directory or archive. A tile number joined a photo to a heat tile; it did not say where that tile lay in Swiss coordinates.

    This left two separate research questions. Which available imagery resembles the supplied photos? And what geographic area should be requested to acquire a replacement? Matching a roof or a forest colour can help with the first question. A reproducible replacement also needs the second, independently of any guess about the original flight year.

    Choosing 2021 as a working reference did not date the supplied photos. Acquiring a new set from explicit 2021 items made that replacement's provenance clearer. The uncertainty about the original copies must stay attached to results using those copies; it should not be transferred to the new set as if its selected source year were unknown too.

    A source resolution is not a tile footprint

    The SWISSIMAGE product name was not enough to infer scale. Multiplying 256 pixels by 10 cm would make each supplied tile only 25.6 m wide. The 65-column, 35-row mosaic would then cover about 1.66 × 0.90 km, implausibly small for the Bern coverage under investigation. The supplied tiles could have been resampled. The source pixel size therefore could not be treated as the final tile's ground sampling distance.

    The first attempts tried to place the stitched photos directly against reference imagery. Normalised cross-correlation of the 65 × 35 mosaic against an 8 km SWISSIMAGE window gave no stable scale or location. The matcher recovered a reference cut-out at exactly its known offset, but on the real mosaic the best score favoured the smallest tested scale and the offsets jumped between scales. A second search used the river-and-lake tile 201 at footprints from 6 to 26 m. Its best and next-best peaks stayed less than 0.04 apart, and the locations still jumped. Neither attempt established a reliable georeference. Those candidate placements were not adopted.

    The later footprint fit used the heat labels and the city's georeferenced 5 m PET raster. The fitting script assembles the numbered label mosaic, bins the raster into the same 14 legend levels, and searches pitch and translation by agreement of valid level cells. It searches coarsely at 10 m, then refines around the best pitch at 5 m. This gives a geographically anchored label-grid fit rather than a flight-date estimate from photo appearance.

    The recorded working placement has a 135.5 m tile pitch, top edge N 1201890, and left edge E 2594060 plus five label pixels in LV95 (EPSG:2056). Here a label pixel means 135.5/256 m, so the eastward correction is about 2.65 m, not five 5 m raster cells. At this placement tile 201 agrees with the binned raster on 99.6% of pixels, and on 92.4% inside the separately checked block. These are agreement checks for that tile and block, not a surveyed positional accuracy or a grid-wide guarantee. The guide's placement comparison shows the check.

    Both replacement builders use the same rule: tile i has row floor((i − 1)/65) and column (i − 1) modulo 65; columns advance east by 135.5 m and rows advance south by 135.5 m. Each requested footprint becomes a 256 × 256 RGB tile. The resulting image scale is about 0.529 m per pixel, even when the source orthophoto is 10 cm. The fitted extent is shared by the imagery and label processing; it is not a recovered world file from the original photos.

    Acquiring the 2021 replacement

    The 2021 builder selects items from swisstopo's open ch.swisstopo.swissimage-dop10 index. For each footprint it identifies all intersecting 1 km squares, requests the item with year 2021 and the square's coordinate identifier, and chooses the 0.1 m TIFF asset. Downloads are cached; interrupted transfers are removed and retried. This is a source-tile download and crop workflow, rather than a screenshot of a web map.

    A Bern tile can cross a source-square boundary. The builder reads each TIFF's top-left tiepoint, crops the intersecting pieces onto a 0.1 m canvas, checks that the canvas is completely covered, then downsamples it to 256 × 256 with Lanczos resampling. A footprint with uncovered canvas pixels is reported and omitted instead of silently extending an edge. The numbered output files go into data/Satellite2021, separately from the originals. The code defines all 2,275 grid positions; its coverage check is a build safeguard, not evidence that the original images shared the same acquisition pipeline.

    The available-year search and the photo comparisons below explain why 2021 was chosen for this acquisition. They do not establish an exact day, season or uniform exposure time for the entire replacement. The later 2020 test also uses a different delivery path, which matters when interpreting a photo-year comparison.

    Imagery · testing the label-year hypothesis

    The 2020 image set did not improve the 2020 target

    The initial selection was a choice among available reference items, not the end of the imagery investigation. On 13 November a request to the SWISSIMAGE product WMS with TIME=2020 returned imagery for tile 201 at the fitted EPSG:2056 footprint. Its mean absolute channel difference from the 2021 response was 17.5 on a 0–255 scale. This ruled out an identical returned image for that check. It did not, by itself, verify each pixel's exposure year or establish uniform dates across the full service mosaic.

    Same footprints, different delivery path

    The new builder requested one WMS 1.3.0 GetMap image per Bern tile from ch.swisstopo.swissimage-product, with TIME=2020, CRS=EPSG:2056, a 256 × 256 output and the same 135.5 m bounding-box grid as the 2021 build. It saved RGB PNGs into data/Satellite2020, preserving the tile identifiers and leaving the 2021 set unchanged. The build notes record 2,275 tiles, no failures, no blank tiles and no wrong-size tiles.

    The geographic request is controlled, but the image-delivery pipeline is not identical. The 2021 set comes from 10 cm TIFF squares assembled and downsampled locally. The 2020 set is rendered by a WMS directly at the requested output size. The experiment therefore compares two acquired photo sets, including their appearance and delivery differences. It cannot isolate calendar year from season, light, product rendering or resampling. Calling the second set “2020” records the requested service time slice; the build does not include an exposure-date audit.

    Why test an earlier image?

    Three difficult forest tiles made the date gap a concrete concern. In tiles 604 and 478, pale, low-height strips read as clear-cut or thinned forest in the 2021 photo. The model predicts too cool over part of 604 and over strips in 478, while tile 281 has the reverse error: a dense stand is predicted too warm. The notes report tile MAEs of 3.45, 2.30 and 2.77 °C for 604, 281 and 478, with local signed errors approaching −8 °C in 604 and +8 °C in 281.

    The 2020 and 2021 photos differ strongly on these tiles: mean absolute colour differences are 35–42 on a 0–255 scale. The clear-cut strips in 604 and 478 are visible in 2021 but not in the 2020 image; 604 also shows more open ground in 2021. This suggested that changed forest cover might put the later photo out of step with the 2020 PET target. It was a testable hypothesis from a few examples, not a measured cause. The same comparison also changes canopy colour and lighting, and the observations do not identify whether felling, storm damage or thinning caused the visible change.

    First swap the input, then retrain

    The first check kept the model trained on 2021 photos and substituted the 2020 photos only at inference. On fold 0 its MAE rose from 1.749 to 1.986 °C over the same 26,025,830 scored pixels. That shows sensitivity to a photo-set change. It does not answer whether training on the earlier photos would help, because the model had never learned their appearance.

    The follow-up trained and scored on the 2020 set. It used the same regression recipe with height, water and a 14:00 shadow layer, seed 0, 60 epochs, the base mask and spatial fold 0. The target remained exact values from the Bern 5 m PET raster, with the same 26,025,830 scored pixels. The saved result records an L1 loss in degrees and a single output channel. This is a regression test against exact temperatures; its scores should not be conflated with the earlier classification comparison between the originals and acquired 2021.

    Retraining recovers about 0.074 °C compared with the inference-only swap, but remains about 0.163 °C worse than the 2021 reference.     What the evidence leaves open

    The 2020 WMS imagery appears more saturated, with leaf-on canopy and different light in the comparison. That may make it a less useful input for this target, but the project has not separated those effects. Nor does a single seed and fold establish that 2021 is the better choice for every split, architecture or area. The result supports keeping the acquired 2021 set for this tested recipe; it is not a universal ranking of acquisition years.

    The original photos' dates remain unknown. The 2018/2021/2024 comparisons describe resemblance, and their mixed winners are compatible with several explanations. The label-based georeference remains a fitted placement with local agreement checks, not recovered acquisition metadata. For the 2020 set, the recorded provenance is the WMS layer and requested time slice, with no per-pixel flight-date verification. For the forest failures, local surface change, seasonal appearance and unexplained model behaviour remain distinct possibilities. The aggregate retraining result does not provide per-tile attribution among them.

    How are the physical input layers constructed?

    The adopted recipe receives eight channels: RGB (three), height above ground, mapped water, and separate shadow estimates at 14:00, 10:00 and 18:00. Layers are sampled on the same tile footprint and read at the same pixel; the exploded picture illustrates alignment, not a sequence of network operations.

    Height: swissSURFACE3D minus swissALTI3D supplies building/tree height above terrain. It describes geometry, not thermal material properties.

    Water: swissTLM3D water polygons supply mapped extent. The 2026 release is newer than the 2021 photos and 2020 PET target, so boundaries and historical changes need care.

    Cast shadow: height geometry and the sun position on 21 June determine geometric shade at three hours. This is computed shade, not a measurement of the photo’s actual illumination and not a full PET simulation. The stack shows the 14:00 example, while the recipe uses three channels.

    Exploratory inputs: bright-flat ground from photo appearance and settlement from VECTOR25 were tested as alternatives. They are not automatically added to the eight-channel headline recipe. Photo-derived colour groups and sky-view factor are separate experiments too.

    Unknown and mismatch flags control which pixels contribute to the loss and metrics. They are not supplied as input channels. Buildings remain unscored.

    Evaluation design

    Splitting by spatial block

    The tiles are neighbours on one map, and neighbours look alike. A random split would put near-twins on both sides and flatter the scores somewhat. Our plan:

    • Cut the 65 × 35 grid into 5 × 5-tile blocks and assign whole blocks at random to train, validation and test (fold 0: 1,149 training / 450 validation / 475 test / 201 buffer tiles (about 50.5 / 19.8 / 20.9 / 8.8% of all tiles)).
    • Use the Large tiles as a measured transfer test: a different scale, with ground truth.
    • Use outside-Bern data to test transfer. Zürich now supplies labels and an initial quantitative check; label and masking differences still prevent direct comparison with Bern. Unlabelled imagery permits visual inspection only.

    The in-Bern test asks "how good on unseen tiles of this city". Outside imagery asks whether the model transfers; quantitative claims require suitable target data and clearly stated protocol differences.

    How should the comparisons be read?

    The comparisons differ in photo version, target, mask, channels, decoder and fold/seed coverage. Headline scores average run MAEs equally; city-map scores weight each valid pixel equally. The displayed three-hour regression map uses seed 1, while its headline combines two seeds.

    Development evaluation, not an untouched final test

    The intended protocol selects settings using validation and reserves testing for the final recipe. Later experiments repeatedly used test performance to retain or reject variants. The reported comparisons remain measured results, but are development estimates subject to adaptive selection. No evidence here establishes that test samples entered training. Independent final validation remains pending.

    Within-one-level and heat-category accuracy must name their decoder too. Some historical tables combine median-decoded MAE with argmax auxiliary metrics; their superscripts preserve that distinction.

    What does “no gain” mean here?

    A small difference on one fold does not prove an idea has no effect. It records why the project did not adopt that variant at that point. Training randomness, difficult regions and repeated test exposure all limit the inference.

    Detailed error investigation · 13–17 November

    From an error map to testable explanations

    The whole-city table above describes several mapped models. The investigations below mostly use regression with 2021 photos, height, water and a single 14:00 shadow input, seed 0, spatial fold 0: 475 test tiles, 26,025,830 scored pixels and 1.749 °C MAE. Three-hour, weighted-loss and other follow-ups are named explicitly.

    Surface subsets overlap, and tile means differ from pooled pixel metrics. A severe error on a small patch can have little effect on citywide MAE. The following analyses revised several early explanations, including canopy-related over-warming and the timing of shade.

    The error is distributed

    The worst ten tiles contribute 4.7% of total absolute error; the worst fifty contribute 17.9%. It takes 174 of 475 tiles to account for half. Fixing a striking example is useful for diagnosis, but is not by itself a route to the overall goal.

    Dark surfaces are a weaker signal

    For luminance below 60, mean bias is −0.24 °C. Underprediction occurs on 303 of 465 evaluable tiles, but only ten have bias below −2 °C. This is a modest aggregate tendency, not an explanation for every large cold region.

    These measurements motivated targeted input tests. Settlement as an extra three-hour-model input reached 1.744 versus 1.716 °C on fold 0; sky-view factor reached 1.725. Neither established a gain. A coarse class can locate much error without containing the information required to correct it.

    Bright-flat surfaces: a severe local failure with limited total weight

    Tile 1065 contains a large pale, smooth surface. The reference PET is cool over that patch, while the model predicts it hot. Tile-average reference and prediction are about 27.1 and 34.7 °C; tile MAE is about 8.4 °C, with local errors of +8 to +12 °C over the basin-like surface.

    The screenshot-derived and source-raster labels agree on 99.7% of the checked valid pixels here. That rules out a palette-decoding discrepancy as the main local explanation; it does not prove that modelled PET and the photographed surface describe the same state. Whether the surface was filled, wet, dry or shaded in the relevant source conditions was not verified. Similar failures across the tested models suggest a shared difficulty, not proof that architecture changes could never help.

    The diagnosis changed as the surfaces were checked

    Early colour summaries called tiles 921 and 139 vegetation failures. Height and settlement checks revised that reading: tile 921 has 58% low/open ground with +3.12 °C bias, versus +0.87 °C on tree-height pixels; tile 139 has 72% open ground with +1.43 °C bias, versus −0.08 °C on tree-height pixels. The coarse settlement layer covers 82% and 100% of these tiles. The evidence points more strongly to open ground in built areas than to a general canopy problem. Settlement is an area class, not proof every pixel is sealed.

    Tile 1130 must not be conflated with 1065. Its hot-predicted area has reference median 35.5 °C and prediction 38.4 °C, an approximately +3 °C difference. A previously quoted +15 °C referred to a small bright-flat subset, not the whole tile.

    Water: useful input, imperfect mask and unresolved narrow channels

    Mapped-water pixels in the fold-0 analysis number 341,669 across 95 tiles, with MAE 2.01 °C and bias −0.42 °C. Tile 201’s water remains about 1.9 °C too cool. Some small-water subsets have much larger errors: tile 605 has 721 mapped-water pixels with about 10.9 °C MAE. These are water-subset scores, not whole-tile MAEs.

    Colour and edge checks exposed ambiguity rather than a single mask defect. Among 80 tiles with at least 200 mapped-water pixels, 64 have vegetation-like colour on most masked pixels. Water can itself be green or bright; that colour rule does not establish that 64 masks are wrong. A −2 px east/west and −7 px north/south image-grid shift improves boundary-edge matching on 11 of 14 checked tiles, but edge alignment does not prove correct shorelines or justify applying a global correction.

    Release year is not observation year

    The rendered WMS is the 2026 swissTLM3D release. A checked 2021-release Bern extract contains river/lake centreline geometries whose reference years range from 2010 to 2018; it is not a set of water polygons observed in 2021. Those reference dates describe the checked archive objects, not every object in the current WMS. Neither release date alone quantifies temporal mismatch or proves that part of the model’s gain comes from it.

    The morning/evening explanation did not hold up

    Of the 105,004 hot-but-14:00-shaded pixels, 73% are also shaded at 10:00, 42% at 18:00 and 26% at all three hours. Only 11% are lit at both other hours. That lit-both group still has MAE 4.83 °C, versus 5.20 °C for pixels shaded at all three times. The single-shadow model stays too cool in both cases.

    Extra shadow hours help, but the timing explanation is unsupported

    Three shadow hours improve overall fold-0 MAE from 1.749 to 1.716 °C and the hot-but-shaded group from 4.547 to 4.134 °C. But the later timing check does not support “these pixels are simply sunlit at another hour” as the explanation. The target is PET at 14:00, not a daily integral. Surface properties, source geometry/date differences and model limitations remain possible, unseparated causes.

    Can we trade average accuracy for a better hot tail?

    A hot-weighted L1 experiment multiplied the loss weight by four for reference values ≥40 °C. It improved the hot subset but shifted the overall map warm. The trade-off is visible only when bias and subgroup KPIs accompany the overall MAE.

    Decoding also changes the trade-off

    A separate saved-softmax classifier check on fold 0 and exact scoring gives argmax MAE 2.823 °C, median 2.451 and probability-weighted mean 2.331. The weighted mean improves average error versus median, while hot-pixel MAE at ≥42 °C worsens from 4.231 to 4.713 °C. This older classifier/checkpoint is not the current 20-run headline or the city-map classifier; do not substitute these figures for that benchmark.

    A coarser prediction did not help

    Post-hoc block averaging of the same predictions raises MAE from 1.749 to 1.865 °C at 9.45 px blocks, and to 2.132 °C at 19 px. This tests smoothing existing predictions, not training a model on a coarser output grid. It does not prove that every coarse-grid architecture would fail.

    The large forest-error regions remain unexplained

    The hotspot search found spatially connected high-error regions, including forest tiles 604, 281 and 478. Their tile MAEs are approximately 3.45, 2.30 and 2.77 °C. Local signed errors approach −8 °C in 604 and +8 °C in 281: the failure goes in both directions.

    Pale low-height strips in 604 and 478 look like thinned or cleared forest. The model stays cool over some patches that the target marks warmer. Tile 281 has the reverse pattern on a dense stand. These examples suggested a photo/target date mismatch, but appearance alone did not establish the mechanism.

    Retraining the single-shadow recipe on the 2020 WMS imagery gives 1.912 °C versus 1.749 °C on acquired 2021 imagery, rather than the predicted improvement. The photo-year explanation was therefore withdrawn as the main account of these forest errors. The delivery paths, saturation, illumination and possible seasonal differences also change, so the experiment cannot assign a zero local effect to genuine surface change.

    The imagery chapter preserves the acquisition, inference-only swap and retraining sequence. The cause of these large errors remains open. Agreement with the source raster rules out one decoding explanation in checked areas; it does not validate every physical assumption in the PET model.

    What the investigation currently supports

    Much error lies broadly in settlement; some bright-flat surfaces fail dramatically but carry little total weight; shadow helps even on hot shaded pixels; water is useful but imperfect; and the largest forest failures do not yet have a tested causal explanation. No single tested explanation accounts for all error clusters.

    Limitations

    What the data cannot tell us

    HighThe hot end is barely reached

    Across the whole city, 0.71% of pixels are at 43 °C or more and 2.88% at 42 °C or more (exact degrees). The mapped classifier predicts about 0.0019% and the independent-threshold model 0.00% at 43 °C or more; Regression predicts 0.05 to 0.08% and is 2.4 to 3.0 °C too cold on the pixels at 42 °C or more. The hot subset has a strong negative mean bias; see the model-by-model hot-end table in Error understanding. The Classification and Ordinal chapters quote 0.77% above 43 °C: that is the share on the 14-level map, which differs a little from the exact map, so the two figures are not interchangeable.

    HighPhoto date and target conditions differ

    The labels are for 2020; the photos are SWISSIMAGE 2021 chosen by date and forest colour. 2021 has structural correlation 0.43 in the shown sample (2018: 0.42; 2024: 0.47), and the original photos match no single year cleanly.

    MediumSingle-seed and partial results

    Original-photo ordinal comparisons use one seed. The current layered regression uses two seeds across five folds; many later tuning and layer-input head tests cover fold 0 only.

    MediumBinning sets a floor

    Scored against exact degrees, any method that predicts bin midpoints has a minimum error of 0.78 °C on tile 201.

    HighPhoto and heat map from different times

    Fields that look green are labelled 40 °C. Seasonal and land-use mismatch may limit reconstruction, especially in the agricultural west; its contribution has not been isolated.

    HighCross-city targets are not yet harmonised

    Zürich provides labelled transfer evidence, but its scale and mask differ from Bern. Harmonised quantitative transfer and reverse-direction evaluation remain open.

    MediumOne city, one map

    2,275 tiles from one city cover few building styles and little topography. The effective number of independent samples is smaller than the tile count.

    MediumPET is not air or surface temperature

    The source product is Bern’s modelled PET Tag 2020 at 14:00. Reconstructing this thermal-comfort index does not establish accuracy for measured air temperature, surface temperature or other weather conditions.

    MediumQuantised and overlaid target

    The historical screenshot target has 14 bins in approximately 9.45 px rendered cells; the current source raster also provides exact degrees on a 5 m grid. Street lines and labels can be painted over, but a few large patches stay unknown. Bin edges are uneven (3 °C wide at the cool end, 1 °C at the hot end).

    MediumImbalanced levels

    23–26 °C covers 37% of the area and > 43 °C covers 0.7%. Extreme heat is the most relevant level and the rarest one.

    MediumBuildings are masked

    Roofs carry no temperature in this layer, so the model never learns anything about them. Predictions on buildings must be masked out at evaluation too.

    MediumBuildings from different dates

    Outlines and photo disagree in places. The flags come from a learned detector (IoU about 0.64), so a few mismatches will be missed and some small real buildings flagged.

    MediumReverse-engineered decoding

    The palette-to-level mapping comes from a colour fit, not from source data, and tiles were exported at different opacities. Each tile's palette is fitted separately. The acquired raw raster removes palette decoding for exact-target runs; screenshot-based historical runs retain this source of uncertainty.

    Generalisation and transfer

    Scale changes and Zürich provide different tests. Target/mask comparability and reverse-direction transfer remain explicit questions.

    What has been tested outside the Bern benchmark?

    Scale transfer

    The supplied Large tiles cover roughly twice the ground width. Their similar class histogram does not establish equivalence: footprint, spatial overlap, resolution and cell rendering differ. A measured transfer experiment must avoid overlap leakage and report these changes. No completed Large-scale benchmark is claimed here.

    Zürich

    The recorded Zürich preparation joins 2025 imagery and 2024 heat tiles, with 95,483 paired tiles. An initial Bern-to-Zürich evaluation reports about 3.13 °C without Zürich training. Zürich therefore does have labels and permits a quantitative check; this score is not directly comparable with Bern because the level definition and mask differ.

    The latest recorded Zürich training attempt crashed and was queued again. This page does not verify its current execution state. Training on Zürich, testing back on Bern, matching the physical target and controlling differences in coverage remain separate tasks.

    Transfer evidence is preliminary

    Distinguish city shift, photo year, label product and masking changes. A labelled second city is necessary evidence, not proof that the Bern recipe generalises.

    Conclusions, reproduction and research record

    What is needed to reproduce the current recipe?

    The original quickstart rebuilds screenshot levels, building/land-cover flags and masks from the supplied image pairs. It does not by itself provision the current layered exact-target benchmark.

    • SWISSIMAGE 2021 cutouts at the fitted Bern tile footprints.
    • The 2020 PET 5 m raster and its exact-degree/level sampling and validity mask.
    • Registered height, water, and shadow channels in the recorded channel order.
    • The saved spatial folds, mask versions, training configuration and inference/decoder settings.

    Some older result files omit photo/target/channel metadata or source revisions. Those gaps remain explicit rather than reconstructed from a convenient filename. A complete checksummed current-data handoff and verified end-to-end rebuild remain open.
