    1Intro
    Result in brief

    A pretrained U-Net predicts the heat map to within about 1.9 °C

    We predict the 14-level heat-stress map of Bern from photos and compare three approaches, named by what the model outputs: Classification (one of 14 classes), Ordinal (13 ordered yes/no outputs) and Regression (one number in °C). With the 2021 photos the best Classification model reaches a mean error of 1.931 °C (median decoding, 20 runs), against 1.978 °C on the original photos. The 2021 photos were better in 20 of 20 paired runs. Ordinal with the CORN head ties Classification on the original photos (1.976 against 1.975 °C). Regression has finished all five folds. The goal was 1.5 °C.

    Three weaknesses shape every number below. The models never predict above 43 °C (0.77% of the true map). The 2021 photos were chosen by date and forest colour and are the weakest year on structure. And photos and labels are years apart, so some of the error is not the model's.

    The brief

    Predict heat islands from photos

    • Train image-to-image models on data from map.bern.ch.
    • Compare the performance of different vision models.
    • Analyse how the models perform outside of Bern.

    Takeaways the supervisors are after: working with image2image models, and evaluating performance and generalisability. Supervisors: Rafael Bischof, Andreas Müller.

    Leakage trap

    A random split over 00All* puts a tile in training and its mirror image in the test set. The scores would measure memorisation. We work from the core set only and flip during training.

    How we analysed it

    Eight passes over the data

    Each pass answered a question the previous one raised.

    1. Colour census

      Counted every RGB value across all 2,275 heatmaps (149 M pixels, 2,311 distinct colours). Twelve colours cover 90% of pixels and the rest are anti-aliasing and drawn-on street lines.

    2. Snap to palette

      Assigned each pixel to its nearest main colour and flagged anything more than 12 RGB units away as overlay. About 95% of pixels per tile snap cleanly.

    3. Order the classes

      First estimate: the mean greenness of the photo under each colour. Then the map.bern.ch legend arrived, and we fitted it directly as a semi-transparent blend.

    4. Reconstruct the layout

      Compared the edge pixels of neighbouring ids. id+1 continues to the right, and id+65 sits directly below in 200 of 200 samples. The tiles form one stitched map.

    5. Level distribution

      Mapped every pixel to one of the 14 temperature levels and compared the core and Large sets.

    6. Fit the rendering per tile

      Tiles were exported with different layer opacities (0.50 to 0.73) and, in three tiles, a darker building grey. Each tile gets its own fitted palette before decoding.

    7. Find the heat raster

      Fitted one grid of cell boundaries over the whole stitched map. 98.55% of cells contain a single colour, so the cells are the true units of the data.

    8. Check buildings against the photo

      Compared the heatmap's building outlines with what the photo shows, in both directions: outlines with no building, and buildings with no outline.

    Finding 1

    The tiles are one continuous map of Bern

    Tile idN sits at row (N−1) // 65, column (N−1) % 65. Stitched together, the 2,275 tiles cover the city from the Wohlensee loop in the west to the Aare bend around the old town. Drag the slider to compare photo and heat map.

    Finding 2

    The pastel colours are the official legend at 60% opacity

    The tiles show softer colours than the legend. Blending each legend swatch with a near-white background (0.6 · legend + 0.4 · 250) and matching it to the observed colours gives a one-to-one assignment with a mean error of 12.5 RGB units. So the target is an ordered temperature scale, not a set of unrelated classes. The five categories in the last column are shown with English names; on map.bern.ch they read keine, schwach, mässig, stark and extrem Wärmebelastung.

    LevelRangeLegendIn tilesFit errorHeat-stress category

    The worst fit is > 43 °C (error 39.8): dark purple loses the most in a blend. It is still the only plausible partner, and it covers just 0.7% of pixels.

    Finding 3

    The temperatures cluster in two groups

    Excluding buildings and overlay pixels, over half of the area sits at 23–29 °C (forest, parks, water edges). A second peak sits at 38–42 °C (sealed surfaces, rail, open fields). The middle levels are thin. This shape decides the loss: a plain mean-squared regression would hedge towards 32 °C where it is unsure, a value that is rare in the data.

    Finding 4

    Where photo and heat map disagree

    Finding 5

    The heat map is a grid of 9.45 px cells, so lines and labels can be painted over

    The heat values were computed on a coarse raster and then drawn with street lines and names on top. Fitting one grid over the stitched map gives cells of 9.45 px in both directions. With that grid, 98.55% of cells hold a single colour.

    The cleaning rule follows from it. A pixel that already matches a legend colour keeps it. A pixel that does not (line, label, anti-aliasing) takes the most common level of its cell. That changes no correctly coloured pixel. Before cleaning, 4.29% of pixels were unreadable; after, 0.077% remain unknown, all in places where the source map has no value.

    Not every tile was rendered the same way

    A first version assumed one palette for all tiles and decoded half of id 382 as unknown, although its buildings are plainly visible. Fitting the layer opacity per tile shows several export batches: 1,411 tiles at 0.64, 360 at 0.55, 96 at 0.63, and a few others. Tiles at the common opacity are unaffected by the fix (0.01% of pixels change). Anyone downloading more tiles later should expect the same variation.

    One raster for the whole city

    The grid's offset moves by about 0.85 px from one tile to the next (256 mod 9.45). The city's heat map was rasterised once and then cut into tiles, so every cell boundary can be computed from a tile's row and column.

    Finding 6

    Some buildings exist in only one of the two sources

    The building outlines in the heat map and the photo were made at different times. Some outlines sit on open fields (planned or newer buildings), and some buildings in the photo have no outline, so the heat map gives them a temperature.

    A first check looked at vegetation under each of the 14,241 outlines. 515 outlines (1.06% of building area) are more than 60% vegetated. That check also catches green roofs, which are real buildings. For a reliable flag in both directions, a small building detector (a U-Net) is trained on the photos in 5 spatial folds, so each tile is judged by a model that never saw its neighbourhood. It reaches a building IoU of 0.62 to 0.66 per fold. Where it confidently disagrees with the heat map, the area is flagged: an outline whose mean building probability is below 0.15, or a region above 0.85 with no outline.

    How flagged areas are used

    Flagged regions and a ring of 6 px around them are excluded from training and evaluation, like unknown pixels. The heat next to a mismatch was modelled with a different building layout than the photo shows.

    Finding 7

    Surface colour carries heat, and some of it disagrees with the photo

    Using simple colour rules on the photo (green vegetation, brown or bare, grey sealed), the heat level of each surface differs a lot. Mean level is 3.6 over green vegetation, 6.5 over grey sealed surfaces and 8.0 over brown or bare ground.

    Two kinds of disagreement stand out. Green and hot: the photo shows vegetation, but the heat is 40 to 41 °C or hotter. This is 7.1% of heat pixels, mostly crop fields. Brown and cool: the photo shows bare ground, but the heat is 26 °C or cooler. This is 0.13%.

    A disagreement is a suspicion, not an error. The heat may have been computed on another date, when the field was bare. The colour rule is also crude: brown catches roofs and soil alike.

    Using it in training

    Two masks are prepared, so each model can choose. The base mask ignores 0.80% of pixels. The land-cover mask also ignores the two conflict types, which brings it to 7.01%. Excluding them removes the farmland examples most needed for learning, so the choice should be tested rather than assumed.

    Manual review: a tile-by-tile viewer shows the flags next to the photo and the heat map. Heat data in those views: Geodaten Stadt Bern.

    Finding 9

    Placing the label grid on the 5 m GeoTIFF

    The city's own raster has 5 m cells, finer than the 9.45 px heat cells in our tiles. To use it, each tile has to be put on the right spot of the GeoTIFF (LV95 coordinates). We fitted the footprint of the tile grid against the raster: pitch 135.5 m, origin at E 2594060 shifted by +5 label px, and N 1201890.

    3Modelling
    Approaches

    Three ways to set up the output, compared on equal terms

    The three approaches are named by what the model outputs. They share the backbone family (a U-Net, with an ImageNet ResNet-34 encoder for the main runs), the split, the photos and the metrics. They differ only in the output and the loss. Two people run them: Timo runs Classification and Regression, Chenjie runs Ordinal. Which approach is used in the final result depends on what the comparison shows.

    Shared protocol

    Split: five spatial folds of 5 × 5 tile blocks, the same for every run (the Split chapter below explains why). Photos: the original photos, and for Classification also the 2021 photos. Labels: the 14 heat levels from the Bern map; Regression can also use exact degrees. Metrics: MAE in °C, accuracy within one level, and accuracy on the five heat-stress categories, which non-specialists can read. Training: 60 epochs, batch 16, AdamW with learning rate 0.002, OneCycle, flips and rotations, smoothed model selection, base mask, RGB input, seed 0 unless the row says otherwise. Every approach is also meant to run with the land-cover mask, so that the effect of excluding the conflicts is measured, not assumed.

    Side tasks, also open: an RGB regression (pix2pix-style) reference, and a search for a photo closer in date to the heat map (SWISSIMAGE 2021 is chosen for now, Bern's own orthophoto is still open).

    Evaluation design

    Splitting by spatial block

    The tiles are neighbours on one map, and neighbours look alike. A random split would put near-twins on both sides and flatter the scores somewhat. Our plan:

    • Cut the 65 × 35 grid into 5 × 5-tile blocks and assign whole blocks at random to train, validation and test (about 70 / 15 / 15).
    • Use the Large tiles as a measured transfer test: a different scale, with ground truth.
    • Use photos from outside Bern (for example swisstopo SWISSIMAGE) for the generalisation task. Without heat maps there, this check is qualitative, unless another city publishes a comparable map.

    The in-Bern test asks "how good on unseen tiles of this city". The outside imagery asks "does it work elsewhere at all". The report needs both, and only the first produces error numbers.

    4Results and comparison
    Approach · Classification

    Classification reaches 1.93 °C with a pretrained encoder and the 2021 photos

    Each pixel gets one of 14 heat levels. Owner: Timo. This is the approach with the most runs: two backbones, two photo sets, four seeds.

    Setup

    • Output and loss: 14 class scores per pixel, cross-entropy.
    • Decoding: argmax is the stored default; median decoding picks the level that minimises the expected error in °C and is the headline number. It lowers the MAE by about 0.14 °C and lowers the within-one-level score by about two points, because the median can sit between bins.
    • Backbones: a U-Net trained from scratch, and a U-Net with an ImageNet ResNet-34 encoder (our decoder, base mask, RGB input, 60 epochs, smoothed model selection).
    • Photos: the original photos, and the SWISSIMAGE 2021 photos at the fitted footprint. Labels are the unchanged 2020 levels.
    • Runs: five folds, and four seeds for the encoder, which makes 20 runs per photo set. The colour-only baseline is kept in every comparison.

    Weak points

    • The hottest level is never predicted. Above 43 °C the model outputs 0.00% of pixels, against 0.77% in the true map. At 42 to 43 °C it outputs 0.15%, against 2.22%. Below 23 °C it outputs 0.35%, against 2.14%. Its extremes are pulled into the middle, so the hottest areas are always underestimated. This is a limit of the model, not noise in the data.
    • The densest tiles are the worst. On the busiest built-up tiles the error is about 1.5 °C higher than in the rest of the city, so the 1.5 °C goal is not met where the city is densest (table below).
    • Photos and labels are years apart. The 2021 photos were chosen by date and forest colour and are the weakest year on structure. Fields that look green are labelled 40 °C, which no photo-based model can learn.
    • Binning sets a floor. Scored against exact degrees, any method that predicts bin midpoints has a minimum error of 0.78 °C (tile 201).

    Approach · Ordinal

    Ordinal: CORN ties Classification, CORAL falls behind

    The model answers 13 ordered yes/no questions per pixel (is the level above k?). Owner: Chenjie. Same protocol as the Classification encoder runs; only the output and the loss change.

    Setup

    Head

    CORAL

    One score per pixel plus 13 shared ordered thresholds, so P(level > k) cannot rise with k. The curve shape is shared, which makes it rigid.

    Head

    CORN

    13 conditional outputs P(level > k | level > k−1), multiplied up. Consistent by construction and more flexible, with less data for the hot end.

    • Output and loss: 13 ordered yes/no outputs, binary cross-entropy. A variant with 13 independent thresholds (no ordering built in) was run by Timo as a check.
    • Decoding: median (count of outputs above 0.5) is the headline; argmax is stored with each run.
    • Protocol: U-Net with an ImageNet ResNet-34, five folds, 60 epochs, batch 16, AdamW with learning rate 0.002, OneCycle, flips and rotations, smoothed model selection, seed 0, original photos, RGB input. Base mask and land-cover mask were both run for CORN.

    Weak points

    • The hot extremes are under-predicted by every run. Level 13 (> 43 °C) is never predicted (0.78% true) and level 12 gets 0.17% (2.22% true), for CORN with the base mask.

    Approach · Regression

    Regression: all five folds done

    One number per pixel in °C. Owner: Timo. The exact-degree target is the new part: it removes the 14-bin floor, but it is scored against exact degrees, which is stricter.

    complete All five folds done.

    Setup

    • Output and loss: one channel in °C, clamped to 21.5 to 43.5, L1 loss in degrees. No decoding.
    • Midpoint target: each pixel's label is the midpoint of its heat level, so this version still carries the binning. Fold 0 only.
    • Exact target: the 5 m GeoTIFF stores a temperature for every cell, not one of 14 bins. Placed on the tiles (Finding 9) it gives exact degrees per label pixel. Any method that predicts bin midpoints has a minimum error of 0.78 °C when it is scored against exact degrees (tile 201).
    • Labels from the raster: levels taken from the 5 m raster agree with the current labels on 84.6% of pixels (92.8% within one level), and the level distributions differ by at most 0.5 points. The rest is mostly raster cells without a value. A classifier on these levels would learn almost the same target, so it was not run.
    • Protocol: same split, ResNet-34 U-Net, 60 epochs, smoothed model selection, base mask, RGB input and seed 0 as the other approaches; original photos.
    • Where the runs stand: fold 0 of the exact target ran on the Mac (7.5 hours under memory pressure); folds 1 to 4 ran on the GPU node. All five folds are in.

    Weak points

    One seed, five folds

    All five folds are in, with one seed. The midpoint target still has fold 0 only, and fold 0 is one of the harder folds (Classification: 2.066 °C median there, against 1.978 °C over all runs), so compare that row with the fold 0 reference.

    • Not directly comparable: with the exact target Regression is scored against exact degrees on 26.0 M pixels in fold 0, Classification against levels on 26.6 M. Exact degrees are stricter.
    • One seed only.
    • Not measured yet: whether Regression predicts the hot end above 43 °C. Exact-degree training is meant to give the extremes a training signal, but this has not been checked on the finished folds. Where a regression is unsure it can blur towards middle values, which the two-peaked distribution punishes.
    • Photos and labels are years apart, as for the other approaches.

    5Conclusion and next steps
    Limitations

    What the data cannot tell us

    HighThe models never predict above 43 °C

    Classification and Ordinal output 0.00% of pixels above 43 °C against 0.77% in the true map; 42 to 43 °C gets about 0.15% against 2.22%. The hottest areas are always under-estimated. Regression has not been checked for this yet.

    HighPhotos and labels are years apart

    The labels are for 2020; the photos are SWISSIMAGE 2021 chosen by date and forest colour. 2021 is the weakest year on structure (correlation 0.43), and the original photos match no single year cleanly.

    MediumBinning sets a floor

    Scored against exact degrees, any method that predicts bin midpoints has a minimum error of 0.78 °C on tile 201.

    MediumSingle-seed and partial results

    The Ordinal and Regression runs use one seed. Regression with the exact target covers all five folds, with one seed.

    HighThe model never predicts above 43 °C

    0.00% of predicted pixels against 0.77% true; 42–43 °C gets 0.15% against 2.22%. The hottest areas are always under-estimated.

    HighPhotos and labels are years apart

    The labels are for 2020; the photos are SWISSIMAGE 2021 chosen by date and forest colour. 2021 is the weakest year on structure (correlation 0.43), and the original photos match no single year cleanly.

    MediumBinning sets a floor

    Scored against exact degrees, any method that predicts bin midpoints has a minimum error of 0.78 °C on tile 201.

    MediumSingle-seed and single-fold results

    The ordinal and regression runs use one seed. The regression results cover fold 0 only, and folds 1 to 4 on the exact target are still running.

    HighPhoto and heat map from different times

    Fields that look green are labelled 40 °C. Seasonal and land-use mismatch sets a ceiling on achievable accuracy, especially in the agricultural west.

    HighNo ground truth outside Bern

    Generalisation to other cities can only be judged visually unless we find a comparable heat-stress map elsewhere.

    MediumOne city, one map

    2,275 tiles from one city cover few building styles and little topography. The effective number of independent samples is smaller than the tile count.

    MediumUnclear physical quantity

    The layer "Wärmebelastung" (heat stress) is given in °C, which suggests a modelled thermal-comfort index such as PET, not measured surface temperature. The layer metadata should confirm the quantity, the time of day and the model used.

    MediumQuantised and overlaid target

    Values exist only as 14 bins in 9.45 px cells. Street lines and labels can be painted over, but a few large patches stay unknown. Bin edges are uneven (3 °C wide at the cool end, 1 °C at the hot end).

    MediumImbalanced levels

    23–26 °C covers 37% of the area and > 43 °C covers 0.7%. Extreme heat is the most relevant level and the rarest one.

    MediumBuildings are masked

    Roofs carry no temperature in this layer, so the model never learns anything about them. Predictions on buildings must be masked out at evaluation too.

    MediumBuildings from different dates

    Outlines and photo disagree in places. The flags come from a learned detector (IoU about 0.64), so a few mismatches will be missed and some small real buildings flagged.

    MediumReverse-engineered decoding

    The palette-to-level mapping comes from a colour fit, not from source data, and tiles were exported at different opacities. Each tile's palette is fitted separately. Raw values from the city would remove this step.
